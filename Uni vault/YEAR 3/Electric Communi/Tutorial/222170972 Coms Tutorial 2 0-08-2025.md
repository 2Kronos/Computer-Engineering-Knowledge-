# Question 1: Benefits of Satellite Communications, Sky Waves, and Space Waves

<u> Satellite Communications</u>

Satellites operate over a wide range of frequencies, such as the Ku-band and Ka-band, with newer systems moving to even higher frequencies to support more users and services. Because they make use of large bandwidths, satellites are able to deliver very high data rates, which is essential for applications like satellite internet, TV broadcasting, and global communication links. Their biggest advantage is providing coverage in areas where terrestrial networks are not practical.

<u>Sky Waves</u>

Sky waves make use of the high-frequency (HF) band, usually between 3 and 30 MHz. These signals are reflected by the ionosphere, allowing communication over very long distances without the need for satellites. However, the available bandwidth is limited, which restricts the data rates. As a result, sky waves are best suited for long-distance but lower-rate communication, such as shortwave radio. The main benefit here is the ability to connect across continents without expensive infrastructure.

<u>Space Waves</u>

Space waves use very high frequencies, such as VHF and UHF, and travel directly in a line-of-sight path. Because of the large bandwidths they can use, space waves are capable of achieving very high data rates, which is why they are the backbone of modern high-speed wireless technologies like Wi-Fi, cellular networks, and microwave links. The key advantage of space waves is their high capacity and low latency over relatively short to medium distances, as long as there is a clear line of sight.

# Question 2: Algorithm for Software-Based Smith Chart Impedance Matching

To build a software version of the Smith Chart for impedance matching, the process can be described in a few logical steps. First, we start by taking in the load impedance ($Z_L$​) along with the transmission line’s characteristic impedance ($Z_0$​). Once these values are available, the load impedance is normalized so that it can be properly represented on the Smith Chart (this is done by dividing $Z_L\ by \ Z_0​$).

The normalized impedance is then plotted on the Smith Chart, giving us a clear visual of where the load lies in relation to the center, which represents a perfectly matched condition. From there, the algorithm simulates the process of adding reactive components such as inductors or capacitors. The aim of this step is to “move” the plotted point across the chart towards the center, which indicates that the impedance has been matched.

Finally, once the right adjustments are determined, the program calculates the exact values of the required reactive components. These values are then presented as the final matching network configuration, providing a clear solution on how to physically achieve impedance matching for the given load.
